MERV vs. MPR vs. FPR: What’s the Difference?

MERV, MPR, and FPR all communicate air-filter particle-capture performance, but they are not three numbers on one universal scale. MERV comes from an industry test standard; MPR belongs to 3M Filtrete; and FPR is The Home Depot’s system. Compare the actual filter—not a random conversion formula.

Quick Answer

MERV is the broad industry rating framework. It reports particle-capture performance from testing under ASHRAE Standard 52.2. MPR is 3M Filtrete’s proprietary rating emphasizing capture of particles from 0.3 to 1 micron. FPR is The Home Depot’s proprietary weighted system for the filters it sells.

These systems overlap enough to support product-specific comparisons, but their numbers are not perfectly interchangeable. Use a MERV value printed or published for the actual product when available. Treat a current manufacturer or retailer crosswalk as that company’s comparison—not a universal equation.

What Is MERV?

MERV means Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value. The value comes from testing under ASHRAE Standard 52.2. The test reports how effectively a filter captures particles in three size groups: 0.3–1 micron, 1–3 microns, and 3–10 microns.

MERV is not the percentage of every particle a filter will remove from a home. It is a laboratory rating based on defined test conditions and particle-size ranges. It also does not state how much air the installed HVAC system will move, how well the frame seals, how long the filter will last, or whether a particular system can accommodate it.

For ordinary residential shopping, the familiar ratings are within MERV 1–16. Higher MERV values indicate stronger required capture performance in the applicable test ranges. If your decision is specifically between common residential levels, the guide to MERV 8, MERV 11, and MERV 13 owns that choice.

What Is MPR?

MPR means Microparticle Performance Rating. It was developed and is used by 3M for Filtrete filters. Current Filtrete guidance says MPR measures capture of particles from 0.3 to 1 micron in air passing through the filter. A larger MPR number indicates greater capture within that proprietary system.

Current Filtrete materials group products into broad MPR bands and frequently place an MPR and a MERV rating on the same product. Those paired labels are useful because they describe that actual Filtrete filter. For example, current Filtrete product pages may identify a product with both its MPR and its tested MERV category.

That does not create a mathematical MPR-to-MERV formula. Product construction and updates matter, and 3M’s own current lineup includes MPR values that are not represented in older internet charts. When comparing a Filtrete filter with another brand, use the MERV value 3M publishes for the specific product rather than interpolating between MPR numbers.

What Is FPR?

FPR means Filter Performance Rating. The Home Depot uses it to rank filters sold through its stores and website. Its current buying guide describes a 1–12 scale built from a weighted average: large-particle capture supplies 60 percent of the rating, small-particle capture supplies 30 percent, and weight gain over the filter’s life supplies 10 percent.

That construction makes FPR more than a renamed MERV number. It is a Home Depot shopping framework with its own inputs and categories. Current consumer guidance groups filters into levels such as FPR 4–5, 6–7, 8–9, and 10.

Home Depot product listings often display both FPR and MERV, but the pairing is product-specific. Current listings show that products in the same FPR tier can carry different MERV labels. That is direct evidence against treating one FPR number as one exact universal MERV conversion.

Can You Convert MPR or FPR to MERV?

You can often find an approximate or product-specific comparison, but you should not use a universal equation. The three systems do not share one scale, one owner, or one identical scoring method.

MPR to MERV

3M publishes MERV ratings alongside MPR for many Filtrete filters and has published a product comparison document. Use those paired values for the actual product. Do not assume an unlisted MPR value falls exactly halfway between two MERV levels, and do not extrapolate beyond 3M’s current lineup.

FPR to MERV

Use the MERV rating on the actual Home Depot listing or product packaging when it is provided. Do not infer an exact MERV solely from the FPR tier. The same FPR category can encompass products with different MERV ratings because the FPR calculation and category boundaries are not the MERV test table.

MPR to FPR

There is no defensible direct formula. If two products use MPR and FPR, respectively, find each product’s published MERV value and compare the actual specifications. If one product does not publish MERV or enough performance information, accept that the comparison is incomplete instead of manufacturing precision.

Why Online Conversion Charts Disagree

Many charts merge historical product pairings, retailer categories, and third-party assumptions into a single grid. Labels and product lineups change, and a store category is not the same thing as an ASHRAE test result. A current primary-source pairing can describe a particular product; it does not establish a permanent universal conversion.

How to Compare Filters With Different Rating Labels

  1. Confirm the physical size first. Record all three nominal dimensions from a properly fitting filter. The rating cannot make a wrong-size filter compatible.
  2. Read the HVAC and filter-cabinet guidance. Look for supported filter dimensions and efficiency information.
  3. Identify the label’s owner. MERV, MPR, and FPR numbers must be read within their own system.
  4. Look for MERV on the actual product. Filtrete and Home Depot listings frequently publish it alongside their proprietary label.
  5. Use only current, attributed comparisons. A 3M pairing applies to the Filtrete product; a Home Depot listing applies to that listed filter.
  6. Compare more than efficiency. Check actual dimensions, depth, construction, replacement guidance, and published pressure drop when available.
  7. Choose for a real filtration need. Do not buy the largest number simply because it looks strongest.
  8. Monitor the installed result. Normal seating and airflow matter after any change.

If the size is unclear, start with how to find your air filter size. If the cabinet accepts different depths, review the differences among 1-inch, 2-inch, and 4-inch filters without assuming depth determines the rating.

Does a Higher Rating Mean More Airflow Restriction?

Not by itself. Efficiency and pressure drop are related design considerations, but the rating number alone does not determine resistance. Media type, pleat geometry, surface area, depth, face dimensions, airflow rate, loading, and the complete filter construction all affect the result.

Two filters with similar efficiency labels can have different published pressure drops. A deeper filter can offer more media area, but only a cabinet designed for that depth can use it. A loaded filter can also become more restrictive regardless of the number printed on the new product.

Use the guide to whether an air filter can be too restrictive for the system-compatibility decision. Do not infer that a high MPR or FPR number is automatically safe—or automatically harmful—without the actual product and HVAC information.

Where HEPA Fits—and Where It Does Not

HEPA is not a fourth shopping number that can be added to a MERV/MPR/FPR conversion chart. It is a different filtration-performance category. A standard central HVAC system should not automatically be assumed to accept true HEPA filtration as a drop-in replacement.

The guide to putting a HEPA filter in a furnace explains cabinet, sealing, airflow, pressure-drop, and system-design considerations. MERV 13 is not HEPA, and neither a particular MPR nor FPR label should be advertised as HEPA through an invented conversion.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between MERV, MPR, and FPR?

MERV is the rating produced by testing under ASHRAE Standard 52.2. MPR is 3M Filtrete’s proprietary rating focused on capture of 0.3-to-1-micron particles. FPR is The Home Depot’s proprietary weighted rating for filters it sells. Their numbers are not one universal scale.

Is MPR the same as MERV?

No. 3M publishes both MPR and MERV labels for many Filtrete products, so those specific products can be compared using their paired ratings. MPR and MERV are still different rating systems and should not be treated as interchangeable numbers.

Is FPR the same as MERV?

No. FPR is The Home Depot’s proprietary weighted system, while MERV comes from ASHRAE Standard 52.2 testing. Some Home Depot product listings show both ratings, but one FPR tier does not necessarily identify one exact MERV value.

Can you convert MPR to MERV?

Use the MERV rating paired with the actual Filtrete product whenever 3M publishes both labels. Do not apply a homemade formula or assume that every MPR number has a universal exact MERV conversion outside the product information.

Can you convert FPR to MERV?

Not with a universal exact formula. Check the actual product listing or packaging for a MERV rating. Current Home Depot listings can pair the same FPR tier with different MERV values, so FPR is better treated as a store comparison category than a precise conversion.

Can you convert MPR directly to FPR?

There is no defensible universal direct conversion. When comparing a Filtrete product with a Home Depot FPR-labeled product, look for the MERV rating published for each actual filter and compare other specifications such as size, depth, and pressure drop where available.

Does a higher rating always mean more airflow restriction?

No. Efficiency rating alone does not determine pressure drop. Media design, pleat area, filter depth, face dimensions, airflow, loading, and product construction all matter. Verify the actual filter and HVAC system compatibility.

Is MERV 13 the same as HEPA?

No. HEPA is a different filtration-performance category and is not another point on an MPR or FPR scale. A standard furnace should not be assumed to accept true HEPA filtration as a drop-in replacement.

Which air filter rating should I use when shopping?

Start with the correct physical size and equipment guidance. MERV is the broadly used comparison framework. If a product emphasizes MPR or FPR, look for its published MERV rating and actual product specifications rather than relying on a generic internet conversion chart.

Does Filter Wizard convert MPR or FPR?

No. Filter Wizard provides a cautious MERV starting point after you confirm the physical size. It does not convert proprietary ratings, measure pressure drop, or verify that an HVAC system supports a particular filter.
